The point isn't a single number. It's that the design was tested against the kind of mess a real lab makes, at the scale Adaptyv runs. We varied the run from 1 week to 8 weeks and 48 to 2,000 designs a week, with normal and bad-week noise, across three random seeds. At 8 weeks and 2,000 designs a week, 5 to 8% of mistakes stay silently wrong with researched noise, about 9% in a bad week; the rest are flagged or heal. Normal noise produced 117 to 162 mistakes with 6 to 11 left silently wrong; bad-week noise produced about 600 with around 50 silently wrong. Usable target always ended slightly under the truth, between 9 and 81 µL, because uncertain tubes are held back rather than counted. What stayed wrong is mostly bins nobody scanned and evaporation nobody records: expressed protein was overstated by 14 to 33 percent. The simulation also tested speed: at four weeks of data the "what's usable" question took 77 seconds, and fixing two lookups that ran once per row made it 1.5 seconds with identical answers. What stays silently wrong is honest: if nobody scans a bin, no system can know. The answer is to make the common case automatic, as we did for single-use plates, and to surface things nobody has seen in a while.

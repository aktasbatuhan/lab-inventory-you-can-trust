# 2026-10-21, day 1

## First read of the case

The line that matters most: "Recording the facts correctly matters more than any single feature." We read the case as a data-integrity problem first and an inventory problem second.

Early ideas we're keeping:
- Never overwrite "how much is left". Record every change as an entry and add them up. Corrections are new entries, not edits.
- Keep two times on every fact: when it happened at the bench, and when someone typed it in. That's how we answer "what did the record say before?"
- Material (what it is), lot (which batch, from where) and container (the barcoded tube or plate) are three different things.
- Material types are rows with rules, not separate tables, so small molecules don't need a schema change.
- Every lab task is a run made of transfers. Some transfers keep the lot the same (splitting a tube); some make a new lot (DNA becomes protein).
- "Usable" is a view that also says why something isn't usable.

## What Batuhan flagged as the key parts of the brief

- Barcodes that people scan
- A scientist may split one tube into many smaller ones
- Containers are thrown away, sometimes with material still inside
- Depth on a few pieces over breadth, and say what we cut and why

## Decision: scenarios first

Write the scenarios before the schema, then turn them into tests. Treat it like making synthetic data: lots of cases, with the kind of mess real people make. Scale comes from a simulation, not from writing rows by hand.

First hand-written draft (20), before the agents expanded it:
1. Target arrives, scanned in, not usable until QC
2. QC pass makes it usable, QC fail blocks it but keeps the record
3. Same delivery registered twice
4. 500 µL tube split into 20 × 25 µL
5. Robot copies a DNA plate into a diluted plate, well by well
6. Plate moves freezer → bench → instrument → back
7. Wrong tube scanned, then corrected
8. Task takes 30 µL from a tube the system thinks has 25 µL (block or flag?)
9. 3 µL left, below what a pipette can get out
10. Plate thrown away with protein still inside
11. Buffer made from 3 components, new lot, short expiry
12. Expression plate: 96 new protein lots, each linked to its DNA
13. Buffer expires today
14. Moved freezer → fridge, expiry clock changes
15. What expires in the next 14 days?
16. How much usable Target X do we have?
17. Target lot was bad: what did it touch?
18. Who changed this tube's volume, and what did it say before?
19. Not scanned in 30 days
20. Physical count differs from the system

Cut on day 1: reservations (out of scope per brief), per-tip tracking, ordering.

## Decisions and the first principle

Batuhan ruled on the eight disagreements the merge surfaced (all in `docs/decisions.md`). Two of his answers went further than the proposal:
- On single-use plates: assume the right action happened, so that when the opposite happens nobody can quietly avoid it and a flag has to be raised. This became a general principle, not a one-off.
- On freeze-thaw limits: set them per protein and source pair, not per material type.

He also set the rule that sits above everything else: every value must be computable by the system. That became principle 1 in `docs/principles.md`. Store facts and rules; work out everything else.

## Schema, tests and the first real design surprise

The schema went from nothing to 20 passing scenario tests in one sitting: three written by hand to prove the idea, seventeen by an agent from the shortlist. The agents weren't allowed to edit the schema; they wrote up what they found instead. That paid off twice:
- Seeded rules were stamped with the load time, so every "what did we know in September?" question saw no shelf-life rules. One agent fixed the seed directly (against instructions, but correctly).
- "A month in the fridge" turned out to be ambiguous once a tube goes back into the freezer. The agent's suggested fix would have made a 20-minute thaw kill a target tube that evening. We chose time budgets instead (decision D9).

## Queries, demo lab, and a bug found by reading the answers

The queries agent built a demo lab that walks the Binding workflow with deliberate mess, and one query per lab question with saved answers. Reading the answers found what the tests hadn't: a lost robot log line left the source well looking fuller than it really was. Fixed by applying principle 3 to robots (decision D10). Four smaller findings fixed too: no rule now reads as "expiry unknown" instead of "fine", single-use plates sitting in an instrument are thrown away with the run, counting a box's tubes confirms the box, and a typed-in move shows as "placed, not confirmed".

Batuhan chose to run the AI-agent lab on his own subscriptions (claude -p and codex exec), with time pressure on, in Python, starting small. A probe proved headless Claude can call our own lab tools; headless Codex can't without approvals, so the referee works from files.
